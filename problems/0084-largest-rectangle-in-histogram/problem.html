84. Largest Rectangle in Histogram Hard Array Stack Monotonic Stack
Key idea: The best rectangle is as tall as its shortest bar, and stretches until a shorter bar on each side. Keep a stack of bars with rising heights. When a shorter bar arrives, pop: the popped bar's right wall is the new bar, its left wall is the bar below it on the stack. A height-0 bar at the end pops everything. O(n).

Problem View on LeetCode (opens in a new tab) ↗

heights[i] is the height of bar i in a histogram; every bar is 1 wide. Return the area of the largest rectangle that fits inside the histogram.

  • 1 <= heights.length <= 10⁵ and 0 <= heights[i] <= 10⁴.
  • The largest possible answer, 10⁵ · 10⁴ = 10⁹, fits in a 32-bit int.

Examples: [2,1,5,6,2,3] → 10 (bars 5 and 6, height 5, width 2); [2,4] → 4.

Edge cases

Try these against your solution before reading the approaches.

Input Expected Why it's tricky
[5] 5 One bar: the rectangle is the bar itself.
[0], [0,0] 0 Height 0 is allowed; the area is 0, not an error.
[1,2,3,4,5] 9 Rising heights: nothing is ever popped during the loop. Without the height-0 bar at the end, the stack is never emptied and the answer comes out 0.
[5,4,3,2,1] 9 Falling heights: every bar pops the one before it right away.
[2,1,2] 3 The best rectangle uses the shortest bar across the whole width. When the stack is empty after a pop, the width is all of 0..i−1; measuring it from the popped bar alone gives 2.
[3,3,3] 9 Equal heights. In Approach 2, at least one pass must pop equal bars (>=). If both passes stop at an equal bar (>), each 3 is boxed in by its neighbours and the answer comes out 3.
[2,1,5,6,2,3] 10 The best rectangle (5 × 2) isn't as tall as the tallest bar (6) or as wide as the whole array (1 × 6 = 6).
10⁵ bars, all 10⁴ 1000000000 The largest answer: 10⁹ still fits in an int, so no long is needed.

Approach 1: Every Pair of Edges

Pick a left edge i, then move the right edge j outward one bar at a time. The rectangle over i..j is as tall as the shortest bar in it, so keep a running minimum instead of recomputing it.

  • Area of i..j = low · (j − i + 1), with low = min(heights[i..j]).
Time O(n²) Space O(1)

Correct, but 5.7 s in Python at only n = 10⁴, and 4.1 s in C++ at n = 10⁵. Too slow for the limits.

Approach 2: Nearest Shorter Bar on Each Side

Turn it around: for each bar, find the widest rectangle that is exactly as tall as it. That rectangle stretches until the nearest shorter bar on the left (left[i]) and on the right (right[i]), so its width is right[i] − left[i] − 1.

  • One pass from the left with a stack: pop every bar that isn't shorter than bar i; what's left on top is left[i].
  • The same pass from the right gives right[i]. Then take the best heights[i] · width.
  • Each index is pushed and popped at most once per pass.
Time O(n) Space O(n)

Easier to get right than Approach 3, since each pass answers one clear question. 44 ms in Python at n = 10⁵.

Approach 3: One Pass with a Monotonic Stack Optimal

Do both sides at once. The stack holds bars with rising heights. When bar i isn't taller than the top, the top bar has found its right wall (i), and its left wall is the bar under it on the stack (shorter, or it would have been popped). Pop it and score height · (i − left − 1).

  • After the last bar, pretend there's one more of height 0 at i = n: it pops everything still waiting.
  • An empty stack after a pop means no shorter bar on the left: left = −1, width i.
Time O(n) Space O(n) for the stack

One pass and no extra arrays: 22 ms in Python and 0.8 ms in C++ at n = 10⁵. The same stack, row by row, solves Maximal Rectangle (85).

In practice No shortcut

No library has a "largest rectangle" function, in Python, C++ or Java. Write the stack.

Remember two things: pop while the top isn't shorter, and add the height-0 bar at the end. In Java, ArrayDeque is the stack to use (Stack is old and synchronized).
