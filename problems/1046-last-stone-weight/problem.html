1046. Last Stone Weight Easy Array Heap (Priority Queue)
Key idea: Every round needs the two heaviest stones, and then a new stone goes back in. That's exactly what a max-heap does: take the biggest in O(log n), add in O(log n). Python's heapq is a min-heap, so store the weights as negatives. O(n log n).

Problem View on LeetCode (opens in a new tab) ↗

stones[i] is the weight of a stone. Each turn, smash the two heaviest together, weights y ≥ x: if they're equal, both break; otherwise the lighter breaks and the heavier becomes y − x. Return the weight of the last stone left, or 0 if none is left.

  • 1 <= stones.length <= 30 and 1 <= stones[i] <= 1000.

Example: [2,7,4,1,8,1] → 1: 8 and 7 leave 1, then 4 and 2 leave 2, then 2 and 1 leave 1, then 1 and 1 break, and the last 1 is left.

Edge cases

Try these against your solution before reading the approaches.

Input Expected Why it's tricky
[1] 1 One stone: no smash at all, it's the answer.
[2,2] 0 Both break and nothing is left. Returning heap[0] without checking crashes on the empty heap.
[3,3,3] 3 Two of them break; the third is never smashed.
[2,7,4,1,8,1] with a plain heapq 1 Forgetting to negate makes it smash the two lightest each time: it says 3.
[9,3,2,10] 0 10 and 9 leave 1; 3 and 2 leave 1; the two 1's break. The new stone must go back with the others, not to the end.
30 stones of 1000 0 An even number of equal stones: they all break in pairs. With 29 of them, one 1000 is left.

Approach 1: Sort Every Round

Follow the rules literally: sort, take the last two, put the difference back, repeat until at most one stone is left.

  • Work on a copy so the caller's list isn't changed.
Time O(n² log n) in theory Space O(n)

With 30 stones this is the fastest here (0.005 ms). The list is nearly sorted after the first round, and Python's sort is fast on that, but it still grows like n²: 108 ms at 10⁴ stones.

Approach 2: Keep the List Sorted

Sort once. Then the two heaviest are always the last two, and the new stone can go straight to its place with a binary search, keeping the list sorted.

  • Python: bisect.insort. C++: upper_bound then insert. Java: Collections.binarySearch, where a negative result -(at) − 1 says where it would go.
Time O(n²): the search is O(log n), but inserting shifts the list Space O(n)

No re-sorting, but each insert moves the elements after it: 16 ms at 10⁴ stones, 1.4 s at 10⁵.

Approach 3: Max-Heap Optimal

A heap gives the biggest item in O(log n) and takes a new one in O(log n), without keeping everything sorted. Each round: pop two, push their difference if it isn't 0.

  • Python: heapq is a min-heap, so push -w and negate again when popping.
  • C++: priority_queue<int> is already a max-heap. Java: new PriorityQueue<>(Collections.reverseOrder()).
  • At most n − 1 rounds, since each one removes at least one stone.
Time O(n log n) Space O(n)

The answer interviewers expect. With only 30 stones it's actually the slowest here (0.010 ms, the negation costs a little), but it's the only one that stays fast as the input grows: 5 ms at 10⁴, 69 ms at 10⁵.

In practice Built-ins

Approach 3 already is the library version: heapq, std::priority_queue and PriorityQueue are the built-in heaps. Python 3.14 also adds max-heap functions (heapq.heapify_max, heapq.heappop_max, heapq.heappush_max), so the negatives aren't needed there.

Older Pythons (and LeetCode, if it isn't on 3.14 yet) don't have them: use the negatives, which work everywhere.
