42. Trapping Rain Water Hard Array Two Pointers Stack
Key idea: The water above bar i is min(tallest on its left, tallest on its right) − height[i]. With two pointers you don't need both maximums: move the lower end inward, because the other side already has a wall at least that tall, so only this side's running max decides the water. O(n) time, O(1) space.

Problem View on LeetCode (opens in a new tab) ↗

Given n non-negative integers that describe an elevation map where each bar is 1 wide, compute how much water it can trap after raining.

  • 1 <= n <= 2 × 10⁴ and 0 <= height[i] <= 10⁵.

Examples: [0,1,0,2,1,0,1,3,2,1,2,1] → 6; [4,2,0,3,2,5] → 9.

Edge cases

Try these against your solution before reading the approaches.

Input Expected Why it's tricky
[1] 0 One bar: there's nothing to hold water on either side.
[1,2,3,4,5] / [5,4,3,2,1] 0 / 0 Only rising or only falling: water runs off the open side.
[2,0,2] 2 The smallest pool.
[2,0,3] 2 Uneven walls: the water level is the lower wall (2), not the higher one.
[3,0,0,0,3] 9 A wide pool: every bar between the walls counts, not just the ones next to them.
[3,1,2,1,3] 5 A bump inside the pool doesn't split it: the water covers the 2 as well, because the outer walls are 3.
[4,2,0,3,2,5] 9 The level isn't set by the nearest walls: bar 2 fills to 4, the tallest wall on its left, not to its neighbour 2.
[0,0,0] 0 All zeros. Also the case [0,1]: a stack solution that doesn't check for a left wall crashes popping an empty stack.
[100000, 0 × 19998, 100000] 1999800000 The most water possible. It just fits in a 32-bit int (limit 2,147,483,647), so int is safe here.

Approach 1: Brute Force (Scan Both Sides for Every Bar)

Water above a bar can rise until it spills over the lower of the two tallest walls around it. So for each bar, find the tallest bar on its left and on its right (each including itself) and take the smaller one.

  • Water at i = min(left_max, right_max) − height[i], never negative because both maximums include bar i.
  • The first and last bars always get 0.
Time O(n²) Space O(1)

The formula is the whole idea; the rest of the approaches only make it cheaper. At n = 20,000 this took 4.6 s in Python here (0.8 s in C++), which risks Time Limit Exceeded.

Approach 2: Prefix and Suffix Maximums

The brute force recomputes the same maximums over and over. Work them all out once: left_max[i] in one pass from the left, right_max[i] in one pass from the right.

  • left_max[i] = max(left_max[i − 1], height[i]), and the mirror image for right_max.
  • Then the same formula, now with two lookups per bar.
Time O(n) Space O(n)

Easy to write and to explain, and the natural first answer in an interview. The follow-up is usually "can you do it without the two arrays?"

Approach 3: Monotonic Stack

Think in horizontal layers instead of columns. Keep a stack of bars that get lower and lower. When a taller bar arrives, the bar on top of the stack is the floor of a pool, the next bar down the stack is its left wall, and the new bar is its right wall.

  • Pop the floor; if the stack is now empty there's no left wall and the water runs off. Otherwise add (i − left − 1) × (min(height[left], height[i]) − height[floor]).
  • Keep popping while the new bar is taller, then push it.
Time O(n) Space O(n)

Each bar is pushed and popped once. Worth knowing because the same "stack of decreasing bars" solves Largest Rectangle in Histogram and Daily Temperatures.

Approach 4: Two Pointers Optimal

Approach 2 needs both maximums only to take the smaller one. Start a pointer at each end. If height[left] < height[right], the right side has a wall at least as tall as anything that matters for left, so its water is just left_max − height[left].

  • Always move the lower end inward, updating that side's running max and adding its water.
  • On a tie, either side works; the code moves the right one.
  • The pointers meet at a tallest bar, which never holds water.
Time O(n) Space O(1)

One pass and four variables. The same "move the lower side" idea as Container With Most Water (11), applied to every bar instead of just the two ends.

In practice Built-ins

There's no "trap water" function, but the running maximums of Approach 2 are a library call in each language: itertools.accumulate with max, std::partial_sum with a max function (forwards and on reverse iterators), and Arrays.parallelPrefix with Math::max.

Time O(n) Space O(n)

Short and passes easily, but it's still Approach 2's two arrays. Java's parallelPrefix only scans left to right, so the right-side maximums need a reversed copy. In an interview, write Approach 4.
