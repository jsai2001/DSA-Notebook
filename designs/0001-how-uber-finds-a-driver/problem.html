How Uber Finds a Driver Geospatial Real-time Matching
Key idea: Don't measure the distance to every driver. Put the map on a hexagon grid (H3), look only in your cell and the 6 around it (then 19, 37, …), keep just each driver's latest location in fast storage, rank candidates by road ETA, and match a whole batch of riders at once to keep the total wait low.

Problem

A rider taps "Request". Find them a good nearby driver within a few seconds.

  • Tens of thousands of drivers can be online in a big city at once, and they keep moving.
  • Every driver's app sends its location every few seconds.
  • Many riders request at the same moment and compete for the same drivers.
  • The match has to be fast, and the pickup wait short.

Thousands of moving drivers, a match in seconds. Play with each step below.

1. The obvious way: check every driver

Take your location, compute the distance to every driver in the city, and pick the smallest. It works, but it does a lot of work per request.

Now scale it up

40,000,000 distance checks for one round of matching, repeated every few seconds as drivers move. That's what melts the servers.

2. Split the planet into hexagons (H3)

Uber's H3 system covers the Earth in hexagonal cells, each under a square kilometre. Every driver's latest location falls in exactly one cell. To find drivers, look in your cell and the 6 touching it. Not enough? Add the next ring: 19 cells, then 37.

Cells per search: 1 → 7 (1 + 6) → 19 (+ 12) → 37 (+ 18). Ring k adds 6k cells, so the total is 3k(k + 1) + 1. Finding a driver's cell is a lookup, not a scan of the whole city.

3. Why hexagons and not squares?

A square has two kinds of neighbours: 4 that share a side and 4 that only touch at a corner, about 1.41× farther away. So "nearby" depends on direction. A hexagon is closer to a circle: all 6 neighbours sit at the same distance.

4. Keep only the latest location

Every driver pings their location every 4 seconds. For matching, only the newest ping matters, so Uber keeps one row per driver in fast storage and overwrites it on each ping.

5. Closest isn't fastest: rank by road ETA

A car 300 m away in a straight line can be across a river and 15 minutes away by road. So candidates are ranked by estimated time of arrival along real roads, not by straight-line distance.

6. Match riders in batches

You and John request at almost the same time. Handing each request its own closest driver can leave someone waiting a long time. Uber collects requests for a short window and matches the whole batch to keep the total wait low.
